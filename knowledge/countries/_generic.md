# How school communications are written, anywhere

- Newsletters mix several weeks: pull every dated item, not only the headline one. "Save the date" lines count.
- The most important facts are often in bold, in a box, in a P.S., or in a table at the bottom. Read tables row by row: a column of dates next to a column of events is many items.
- Money: "due" means a payment deadline. Trip letters usually carry three items: the trip (event), the slip (consent) and the fee (payment).
- Consent language: "permission slip", "consent form", "sign and return", "reply slip", "tear-off", "autorisation", "Einverständniserklärung", "동의서", "承諾書 / 同意書". A tear-off section at the bottom of a letter always means consent.
- Pickup and dismissal changes ("early dismissal", "half day", "late start", "no after-school club") are events parents must plan around.
- "No school", "closed", "holiday", "inset / PD / teacher training day" are all-day events; multi-day breaks use end_date.
- A weekly pattern (PE days, library day, show-and-tell letter, reading log) becomes one item whose description states the rule.
- Group-chat and app messages (WhatsApp, KakaoTalk, LINE, ClassDojo, Remind, Seesaw, ParentSquare, Bloomz) are informal: dates may be relative ("tmrw", "this Fri"); resolve from the message date when given, else today, with confidence "medium".
- Screenshots of apps: the message timestamp at the top is the reference date for relative words.
- Forwarded e-mails: the original sender inside the forwarded block is the sender; ignore the forwarding parent's signature.
- Siblings: if a notice is from a specific class or grade and a child's name is not written, leave children empty unless the parent gave a hint.
- When a date is written in words and in numbers and they disagree, trust the words and add a question for the parent.
